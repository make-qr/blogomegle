How to Choose a Wife or Husband You'll Still Like in 10 Years

By Morgan Rivers, Staff Essayist · Love Journey · July 28, 2026 · 5 min read

Falling in love is easy. Choosing someone you will still genuinely like a decade from now takes different criteria entirely. Here is what actually matters.

Video: The Gottman Institute — the research-backed habits that predict lasting marriages

On this page

    Loving someone at twenty-six and still genuinely liking them at thirty-six are two different achievements. Love, in the early sense, is remarkably easy to generate — proximity, chemistry, and a few good conversations can produce it in weeks. Liking someone across a decade of ordinary Tuesdays, financial stress, family drama, and both of you changing as people is a much harder thing to predict — and a much better thing to select for.

    Most people choose a spouse based on how a relationship feels in its first year. This guide, part of our Love Journey series, is about choosing based on how a relationship is likely to feel in its tenth.

    Why early chemistry is a poor predictor of long-term liking

    Early relationships run on novelty, mutual performance, and low-stakes interactions. You have not yet split rent, argued about parenting styles, negotiated in-laws, or nursed each other through illness. Chemistry tells you whether you are drawn to someone. It tells you almost nothing about whether you will handle a decade of real life well together.

    This is not cynicism about romance — passion matters and deserves respect. It simply is not sufficient information for a decision this large. Marriages that last and stay genuinely warm are usually chosen on a second, quieter set of criteria layered underneath the chemistry.

    Criterion 1: How they handle being wrong

    Watch closely, early and often, for how a person responds to being corrected or shown they made a mistake. Do they get defensive, deflect blame, or shut down? Or can they say “you’re right, I got that wrong” without a spiral of shame or a counterattack?

    Over ten years, you will both be wrong constantly — about small things and large ones. A spouse who can absorb being wrong without collapsing or attacking is someone you can actually build a life with, because repair stays possible.

    Criterion 2: Their relationship with their own family

    You are not just marrying a person; you are marrying their history. Watch how they talk about parents and siblings — not to judge the family itself, but to see the patterns your partner may repeat or resist. Someone who has done real work understanding their family dynamics tends to bring more self-awareness into marriage than someone who has never examined it.

    This also predicts holidays, boundaries, and money conversations for the next several decades, so it is worth taking seriously early rather than discovering it after the wedding.

    The quiet, unremarkable conversations — not the big romantic gestures — reveal who you’re actually choosing.

    Criterion 3: Compatibility under stress, not just under ease

    Anyone can be pleasant on a good vacation. The real test is how someone behaves when they are tired, sick, broke, or scared. Do they get meaner under pressure, or do they stay basically the same person with less patience? Do they pull toward you under stress, or push you away?

    Before choosing a spouse, try to genuinely observe them during at least one hard stretch — a family emergency, a job loss, a health scare, a stressful trip. It reveals more in a week than months of easy dating.

    Criterion 4: Shared direction, not identical hobbies

    You do not need to like the same movies or the same weekend activities. You do need roughly aligned direction on the handful of things that shape a decade: whether and how many children, where you want to live, how you handle money, how ambitious each of you wants to be professionally, and what role faith or spirituality plays.

    Misalignment here does not always end a relationship, but it requires honest, early conversation — not hopeful assumption that things will “work themselves out” after the wedding.

    Criterion 5: Whether respect survives disagreement

    Contempt — eye-rolling, mockery, sarcasm meant to wound — is one of the most well-documented predictors of relationships that sour over time. Passion can fade and be rebuilt. Respect, once it curdles into contempt, is much harder to recover.

    Notice how a potential spouse talks about you when frustrated, and how they talk about ex-partners. If ridicule is a habitual tool rather than a rare lapse, take that seriously as a preview of your future arguments.

    Criterion 6: Humor that includes both of you

    Couples who stay genuinely fond of each other after a decade usually keep some shared humor — private jokes, an ability to laugh at themselves, lightness that survives stress. Humor at the other person’s expense, used to control or belittle, is different and corrosive. Humor shared with each other tends to protect a marriage against the slow erosion of resentment.

    Shared, gentle humor in ordinary moments is one of the strongest predictors of a marriage that stays warm.

    A conversation worth having before the wedding, not after

    Before committing long-term, it helps to have direct conversations about:

    • How we will handle money, separately and together.
    • What roles we expect around housework, career, and eventual children.
    • How we each fight, and what we need during and after conflict.
    • What we each learned — good and bad — from our parents’ marriages.
    • What “success” looks like for us individually and as a couple in ten years.

    These conversations are rarely romantic, but they are exactly the ones that predict whether you will still like each other once the initial excitement settles into ordinary life. If you want a broader lens on what “real love” even means before applying these filters, our piece on what true love actually means is a useful companion read.

    Practicing honest communication before marriage

    None of these criteria matter if you cannot actually talk to your partner clearly. Practicing plain, low-drama communication — in texts, in person, during disagreements — is a skill you can build well before a wedding. Our guides on texting without overthinking and starting real conversations both build the same muscle marriage will lean on for decades: saying true things clearly, without needing a perfect script.

    If you’re choosing out of fear of being alone

    Some people rush a spousal choice because loneliness has become unbearable rather than because the match is genuinely right. That is worth being honest with yourself about. If evenings feel too quiet, practicing easy, low-pressure conversation with new people — for example through OmegleChat’s free browser chat — can ease the loneliness enough that you are choosing a spouse from clarity, not from urgency.

    Closing

    Choosing someone you will still like in ten years is a different project than falling for someone today. It means watching how they handle being wrong, how they treat their family, how they behave under stress, and whether respect survives your worst days together. Passion is a wonderful beginning. These quieter criteria are what keep a marriage genuinely warm long after the wedding photos fade.
