When dinner becomes an argument

By Morgan Rivers, Staff Essayist · Table Talk · November 20, 2026 · 1 min read

How to stop a meal from turning into a trial, and how to come back to the table without pretending nothing happened.

On this page

    Dinner is a bad courtroom. You are hungry, you are facing each other, and the food goes cold while someone tries to win.

    If the tone changes, name it early. “This is becoming a fight. I want to eat first.” Then do that. Put the topic on a note if you are afraid it will vanish. A note is not avoidance when you also name a time. “After the dishes, twenty minutes, then we stop.”

    During those twenty minutes, one person speaks for two minutes without the other correcting details. Then you switch. Details can be wrong and the feeling can still be real. Start with the feeling. “I felt dismissed when you checked your phone” is usable. “You always ruin dinner” is a closing argument.

    Do not bring in parents, exes, or a list from March unless that list is the actual subject. Old exhibits make the person defend their whole character instead of this evening.

    If someone cries or goes quiet, offer water and a pause, not a verdict. “We can stop” is a complete sentence. Leaving the room to cool off is fine if you say when you are coming back. Disappearing until tomorrow teaches the other person that the meal was a trap.

    When you return, do not require a cheerful recap. Eat something, even if it is toast. Then one repair: “I was sharp about the phone. I still want to hear the rest tomorrow.” A repair is specific. “Sorry you feel that way” is a second argument wearing a polite coat.

    Some dinners should be small on purpose. Soup, a walk, bed. A hard week does not need a feast and a summit on the same night.
