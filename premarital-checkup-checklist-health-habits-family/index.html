Premarital Checkup Checklist (Health, Habits, and Family)

By Morgan Rivers, Staff Essayist · Love Journey · August 07, 2026 · 6 min read

A premarital checkup is bigger than one doctor's visit. Here is a practical checklist for talking about health, daily habits, and family history before marriage — with honesty, not panic.

Video: Robert Waldinger (TED) — what makes a good life: the quality of our close relationships

On this page

    When people hear “premarital checkup,” most picture a single clinical appointment — bloodwork, a form, maybe a vaccine record. That is one small piece of a much bigger conversation. A real premarital checkup includes physical health, mental health, daily habits, and family history — the quiet variables that shape a marriage far more than the wedding day ever will.

    This checklist is part of our Love Journey series, and it pairs naturally with Money talks before marriage and Building a home that feels safe for both of you — three unglamorous conversations that quietly decide how steady a marriage feels five, ten, and thirty years in.

    Please read this important note first: this article is educational relationship writing, not medical advice. It is meant to help you organize honest conversations with your partner and know what to bring up with real clinicians — not to replace an actual doctor, genetic counselor, therapist, or other licensed professional. Any specific health question, symptom, or diagnosis belongs in front of a qualified provider, not in a blog checklist.

    Why a broader checkup matters

    Couples often discover major health or habit differences only after the wedding — not because anyone lied, but because nobody thought to ask. A structured premarital conversation reduces painful surprises and helps both partners walk into marriage with open eyes rather than assumptions.

    The goal is not to interrogate each other. It is to build the same kind of honesty that makes every other part of a relationship work: say the true thing before it becomes a hidden thing.

    Section 1: Physical health conversations

    These are starting points for a conversation with your partner — and, where relevant, with your own doctors. This is general educational information, not a diagnosis or treatment plan for anyone.

    Topics worth discussing openly:

    • Any chronic conditions, allergies, or ongoing treatments the other should know about
    • Family planning intentions and any known fertility considerations
    • Medication routines that affect daily life (timing, side effects, cost)
    • General checkup history — has each of you actually seen a doctor recently?
    • Any conditions that run in either family that a doctor should know about when planning a family

    If either of you has specific concerns — fertility, a chronic illness, a genetic condition — the right next step is a real conversation with a physician or genetic counselor together, not guesswork from articles online.

    A real medical conversation belongs with a licensed provider — this checklist just helps you know what to bring up.

    Section 2: Mental health honesty

    Mental health deserves the same directness as physical health, without stigma attached:

    • Has either partner experienced depression, anxiety, or other mental health conditions?
    • Is either partner currently in therapy, or open to it if needed later?
    • How does each partner typically behave under high stress — withdrawal, irritability, needing space, needing closeness?
    • What has helped in the past during hard mental health periods, and what has made things worse?

    None of this is meant to be diagnostic. It is meant to remove guesswork later, so that if a hard season arrives, neither partner is caught completely off guard by how the other copes.

    Section 3: Daily habits that shape a marriage

    Small daily habits often cause more day-to-day friction than big life events. Worth discussing honestly:

    • Sleep — schedules, snoring, screen use in bed, morning vs. night preferences
    • Substances — alcohol, smoking, or other habits, including how each partner feels about the other’s use
    • Exercise and movement — expectations around fitness, and whether it is shared or separate
    • Food — dietary needs, restrictions, and how meals get planned and cooked
    • Screen and work habits — how much of the evening realistically goes to phones, laptops, or after-hours work

    These habits rarely feel important during dating, when time together is limited and curated. They become the actual texture of daily married life — worth naming now rather than discovering slowly through irritation.

    Section 4: Family health history

    A calm conversation about family history helps both partners plan ahead, especially around future family planning:

    • Any hereditary conditions on either side of the family
    • Family patterns around chronic illness, mental health, or substance use
    • How each family has historically handled aging parents and long-term caregiving

    Again: this is a conversation-starter checklist, not a diagnostic tool. If specific hereditary risks come up, a genetic counselor or physician is the right next step — they can offer accurate, personalized guidance that no article can responsibly provide.

    Section 5: Family dynamics and expectations

    “Family history” is not only medical — it is also about how each family operates:

    • How does each family handle conflict — directly, or through silence and avoidance?
    • What are the unspoken expectations around holidays, visits, and hosting?
    • Are there financial support expectations toward parents or siblings, and does each partner know about them? (This connects directly with the family-pressure script in Money talks before marriage.)
    • What does each family consider “normal” that the other might find surprising?

    Many marital conflicts about in-laws are really conflicts about unspoken defaults — each partner assuming their own family’s normal is universal. Naming these defaults out loud early prevents years of quiet friction.

    Most of this checklist is simply permission to ask questions you have been avoiding.

    A simple premarital checklist you can use tonight

    Go through this together, at a relaxed pace, over more than one sitting if needed:

    1. Have we both had a general checkup recently, and do we know each other’s basic health picture?
    2. Have we talked honestly about mental health history and coping styles under stress?
    3. Do we know each other’s real daily habits — sleep, substances, screen time, exercise?
    4. Have we discussed family health history relevant to future family planning?
    5. Do we understand each other’s family dynamics, expectations, and support obligations?
    6. Is there anything either of us has been avoiding saying because it feels too vulnerable?

    You do not need identical answers on every line. You need visibility — no major hidden variables walking into a shared life.

    When to bring in a professional

    This checklist is a conversation starter, never a substitute for real expertise. Bring in a professional when:

    • Either partner has an unresolved chronic health condition or new symptoms
    • Family planning involves known genetic risk factors
    • Mental health history includes trauma, severe depression, or ongoing treatment needs
    • The couple wants structured premarital counseling rather than a self-guided checklist

    A good physician, therapist, or premarital counselor can do far more with your specific situation than any general article, including this one.

    Closing

    A premarital checkup is really a permission slip: permission to ask the questions you have been quietly avoiding because they feel too clinical or too vulnerable for a romantic relationship. In reality, this kind of honesty is romantic — it says, I want the real version of you and the real version of us, not the curated one. Paired with the money and home conversations elsewhere in this series, it forms a fuller, more honest foundation than a wedding day alone can provide, echoing the deeper idea in What true love actually means.
